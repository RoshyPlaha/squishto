If you've noticed your Microsoft Teams meeting invites look messier than they used to, you're not imagining it. In January 2026, Microsoft finished rolling out a change that shows the meeting link itself, in plain text, right in the invite - instead of hiding it behind a clean "Join the meeting now" button. So even though Microsoft also shortened the underlying URL format around the same time, the net effect for a lot of people is the opposite of what it sounds like: you now *see* a long link where you used to see a short label. Here's what actually changed, and a simple way to shorten a Microsoft Teams meeting link yourself so it's something you can actually text, print, or read out loud.

## What Microsoft actually changed

Per Microsoft's own Message Center update (MC772556), Teams moved from meeting URLs shaped like `teams.microsoft.com/l/meetup-join/19%3ameeting_...` - a long string carrying your tenant ID, organizer ID, and conversation ID - to a shorter format: `teams.microsoft.com/meet/<meeting_id>?p=<hashed_passcode>`. Microsoft's stated reasons were usability and security: shorter links, and less identifying information exposed in the URL itself.

The rollout reached General Availability worldwide in January 2026 (GCC High and DoD environments followed in February). Alongside it, invitations started displaying that link as visible text instead of a "Join the meeting now" hyperlink - which is the part people have actually been noticing and asking about.

Whether this display change applies to every meeting type (scheduled meetings and ad-hoc "Meet Now" meetings alike) or just one isn't fully consistent across sources describing the rollout - so if you're troubleshooting a specific invite, check your own Teams admin settings rather than assuming.

## Why the "shorter" link still isn't short enough to share

Here's the catch: "shorter" is relative. The new format trims out tenant and conversation identifiers, but what's left is still a machine-generated meeting ID plus a hashed passcode - not something anyone is going to read aloud on a call, retype from a whiteboard, or recognize at a glance in a text thread. Paste one into a Slack DM or a text message to a client and it still wraps across two or three lines and looks, frankly, a little suspicious.

That's a fine trade-off for a calendar invite, where you're clicking, not reading. It's a worse one for anywhere you're sharing the link outside the invite itself - a text to someone joining from their phone, a printed agenda, a one-line email to an external partner who isn't in your calendar system.

## The fix: shorten it yourself before you send it

If you're sharing a Teams link somewhere other than the original calendar invite, run it through [squish.to](https://squish.to) first and give it a slug you'd actually want to read out loud: `squish.to/tuesday-standup` instead of a 60-character string with a hash in it. It takes about ten seconds:

1. Copy the meeting link from Teams (the "Copy meeting link" option in the meeting details).
2. Paste it into squish.to and pick your own ending instead of a random one - something like `squish.to/team-standup` or `squish.to/client-kickoff`.
3. Share that instead - over text, on a printed agenda, or read out loud on a phone call.

No account required, and every link comes with click tracking built in, so if you're the one setting up a recurring external meeting, you can also see whether anyone's actually clicked before the call starts.

One honest caveat: Teams meeting links expire - 60 days after the meeting for scheduled meetings, and much sooner for ad-hoc "Meet Now" links. A squish.to slug just redirects to whatever URL you gave it, so it won't outlive the Teams link underneath it. Create a fresh one per meeting rather than trying to reuse the same slug for a recurring series with a rotating link.

If you're building a slug you'll actually say out loud on a call - "go to squish dot to, slash tuesday, dash standup" - it's worth applying the same design test we cover in [how to build a short link you can say out loud](/blog/short-links-you-can-say-out-loud): avoid characters that are ambiguous when spoken, and watch for words that accidentally merge into something else when the dashes disappear.

For reference, squish.to custom slugs run 3-30 characters, using letters, numbers, and hyphens - no underscores - and they're case-sensitive, so keep candidates lowercase to avoid any ambiguity when you're reading one aloud.
